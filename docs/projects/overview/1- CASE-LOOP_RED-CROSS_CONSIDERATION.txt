# Batch 1 — Executive summary and strategic direction

## 1\. Executive summary

CaseLoop is a proposed lightweight humanitarian response coordination and operational intelligence application.

It is intended to support the administrative and coordination activities that occur around humanitarian response operations, particularly the management of assignments, response teams, supporting personnel, operational updates, outstanding tasks and management summaries.

The product is designed around a simple observation from the Karen-Lang'ata branch environment.

Based on the high-level workflow described during the attachment, Red Cross members communicate through an existing group. The Governor receives information, assigns a response team and coordinates with nearby support. The Governor also handles reporting and other coordination responsibilities.

This suggests an opportunity to develop a supplementary application that helps maintain structured records of these activities.

It does not establish that the existing workflow is inadequate, that reporting is unreliable, or that the organization lacks an official system.

CaseLoop therefore adopts a deliberately non-disruptive approach.

It should:

- Complement existing communication channels.
- Allow authorized users to record and track response assignments.
- Maintain a structured operational timeline.
- Provide visibility into outstanding activities.
- Generate useful operational summaries.
- Support offline use.
- Offer optional local AI assistance.
- Allow future integration with approved organizational systems.

The system should remain useful even if its AI capabilities are disabled, its users prefer existing reporting procedures, or management decides not to adopt it.

### The central product proposition

**CaseLoop gives humanitarian response teams a structured operational notebook that can grow into a coordination and intelligence platform when organizational needs justify it.**

This is the product's most important positioning decision.

It avoids presenting the application as a replacement for established organizational infrastructure and makes experimentation easier to accommodate.

## 2\. Vision, mission and objectives

### Vision

To make humanitarian response coordination more accessible, traceable and adaptable through practical, offline-capable and open-source-friendly technology.

### Mission

To develop a modular software platform that helps humanitarian teams organize operational information, maintain response continuity, coordinate activities and generate useful insights while respecting existing organizational processes.

### Strategic objectives

1. Develop a working operational coordination prototype.
2. Minimize disruption to existing workflows.
3. Improve visibility into response assignments and outstanding activities.
4. Provide structured operational histories.
5. Reduce repetitive administrative work through automation.
6. Introduce optional local AI capabilities.
7. Create an extensible software foundation.
8. Establish documentation that enables future developers to continue the project.
9. Explore future applicability to other humanitarian organizations.
10. Develop a credible independent product opportunity without misusing organizational resources.

### What CaseLoop should not become

These exclusions are as important as the proposed features.

CaseLoop should not initially attempt to become:

- A replacement for WhatsApp.
- A national emergency dispatch system.
- A complete Red Cross enterprise resource planning system.
- A replacement for official incident reporting.
- A beneficiary database.
- A public complaint or safeguarding management system.
- An autonomous emergency decision-making system.
- A mandatory organizational platform.

Trying to become all these things would introduce unnecessary complexity and create adoption barriers.

CaseLoop should be positioned as a product that can exist at three levels.

\<box border radius="lg" padding={3} gap={3}\> \*\*Level 1 — Operational notebook\*\*

\<badge color="success"\>Initial MVP\</badge\>

Record assignments, response details, updates, team responsibilities and outstanding actions.

\<divider color="subtle"/\> \*\*Level 2 — Coordination companion\*\*

# Batch 3 — Functional system analysis and requirements

## 8\. Functional requirements

The functional requirements define what the system must actually do.

For the first version, I recommend prioritizing reliable record keeping and coordination rather than advanced analytics.

### 8.1 User and access management

The application should eventually support different user roles.

| Role | Permissions |
| --- | --- |
| Administrator | Manage users, settings and system configuration |
| Coordinator | Create, assign and oversee responses |
| Team leader | Acknowledge assignments and submit team updates |
| Team member | View authorized assignments and record updates |
| Observer | Read authorized operational summaries |

For the first demonstration, create fictional accounts for each role.

Do not implement a complicated organizational identity-management system before the workflow is validated.

### 8.2 Response register

This is the central module.

Each response should have:

- Unique response reference.
- Response title.
- Description.
- Response category.
- Date and time created.
- General location.
- Priority.
- Current status.
- Responsible coordinator.
- Assigned team.
- Supporting teams.
- Relevant deadlines.
- Creation and modification timestamps.

Suggested response categories:

- Disaster response.
- Community assistance.
- Emergency support.
- Preparedness activity.
- Logistics support.
- Training or exercise.
- Other.

Categories should be configurable rather than hardcoded permanently.

### 8.3 Assignment management

Each response can contain one or more assignments.

An assignment should include:

- Assignment reference.
- Response reference.
- Assigned team or officer.
- Assignment description.
- Date assigned.
- Expected completion date.
- Acknowledgement status.
- Progress status.
- Supporting resources.
- Completion notes.

Suggested statuses:

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Draft | Not yet issued |
| Assigned | Responsibility allocated |
| Acknowledged | Recipient has confirmed receipt |
| In progress | Work has commenced |
| Awaiting support | Additional assistance required |
| Completed | Assigned work reported complete |
| Cancelled | Assignment withdrawn |

Do not treat an assignment as completed merely because somebody changes its status. Record who completed it and when.

### 8.4 Operational timeline

This module preserves the history of a response.

Example:

| Time | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 08:30 | Response record created |
| 08:45 | Team assignment issued |
| 09:05 | Team acknowledges |
| 09:40 | Initial progress update |
| 10:15 | Additional support requested |
| 11:20 | Support confirmed |
| 13:00 | Team submits completion update |

The timeline should be chronological and auditable.

Important events should not simply disappear when a record is edited.

### 8.5 Resource and support requests

This is a useful extension of the coordination workflow.

A response team may need:

- Transport.
- Equipment.
- Personnel.
- Supplies.
- Technical support.
- Additional logistical assistance.

CaseLoop could record a request, its status, who is responsible for following it up and whether it was fulfilled.

For the MVP, this can be a simple resource-request register. Full inventory management is unnecessary.

### 8.6 Reporting and exports

The application should support:

- Individual response summaries.
- Assignment status reports.
- Outstanding action reports.
- Response activity timelines.
- Resource request summaries.
- Date-range reports.
- CSV exports.
- Printable HTML reports, with PDF export added where feasible.

The goal is to reduce repetitive compilation, not create a competing official reporting format.

### 8.7 Management dashboard

The initial dashboard should answer five questions:

1. How many responses are currently being tracked?
2. Which assignments are outstanding?
3. Which activities are overdue?
4. What support requests remain unresolved?
5. What has happened recently?

Recommended dashboard components:

- Active response count.
- Completed response count.
- Pending assignments.
- Overdue activities.
- Outstanding support requests.
- Recent operational activity.
- Response category distribution.
- Response duration trends.

Avoid creating performance rankings of individual volunteers. Such metrics could be misleading and discourage participation.

### Important data design decisions

**Use unique identifiers.** A response reference should not depend on its title or location.

**Use timestamps consistently.** Store timestamps in UTC internally and display them in the appropriate local time zone.

**Separate assignments from responses.** One response may have several assignments, and each assignment may have a different status.

**Preserve history.** Important updates should generate events rather than overwrite the entire record.

**Use soft deletion where appropriate.** Operational history should not disappear through accidental deletion. Retention and deletion rules must nevertheless be configurable and comply with organizational requirements.

**Keep sensitive information out of the MVP.** Do not store beneficiary identity documents, sensitive case narratives or safeguarding records.

# Batch 5 — Offline architecture, AI and security

## 14\. Offline-first system design

Offline capability is one of the most meaningful aspects of the proposed product.

However, there are different levels of offline operation.

### Level A: Single-device offline

\<badge color="success"\>Initial implementation\</badge\>

The application runs on one computer.

- Frontend and backend run locally.
- SQLite stores application records.
- No internet connection is needed for core operations.
- AI can run locally if a compatible model is installed.
- Reports can be exported to local files.

This is the best starting point.

### Level B: Local network operation

\<badge color="info"\>Second phase\</badge\>

The application runs on one approved computer acting as a local server.

Other authorized devices access it through the same local network.

For example:

text

```
               Local server
                    |
             CaseLoop backend
                    |
               Database
                    |
          Local Wi-Fi network
             /          \
            /            \
       Computer A     Computer B
            \            /
             \          /
              Computer C
```

This enables multiple users to work with a common dataset without relying on internet connectivity.

It introduces additional requirements:

- Server authentication.
- Network access restrictions.
- Device authorization.
- Backups.
- Concurrent editing.
- Server availability.
- Local network security.

Do not assume a local Wi-Fi network is automatically secure.

### Level C: Offline-first synchronization

\<badge color="discovery"\>Future development\</badge\>

Users can work independently and synchronize their changes when connectivity becomes available.

This is significantly more complicated.

For example:

- User A changes an assignment to `in progress`.
- User B changes the same assignment to `awaiting support`.
- Both are offline.
- Their devices later reconnect.

The system needs an explicit conflict-resolution strategy.

My recommendation is to preserve both changes as events and require review when the conflict affects important operational information.

Never silently apply a last-write-wins policy to critical response decisions.

## 15\. Local AI strategy

The AI architecture should be modular.

It should be possible to use CaseLoop without AI, with a local AI model, or with an approved external AI provider.

### 15.1 AI capabilities

| Capability | Priority | Proposed approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Response summaries | High | Local LLM |
| Report drafting | High | Local LLM |
| Action extraction | Medium | Rules + local LLM |
| Feedback categorization | Medium | Rules initially, optional model |
| Document search | Medium | Local search + retrieval |
| Operational forecasting | Low | Defer |
| Autonomous assignments | Excluded | Human decision required |

### 15.2 Recommended model approach

Start with Ollama or llama.cpp.

Do not commit to a particular model until you test the actual hardware.

Potential model families to evaluate include:

- \<Entity value="Qwen" category="software" disambig="Open-weight language model family"/\>.
- \<Entity value="Llama" category="software" disambig="Open-weight language model family"/\>.
- \<Entity value="Gemma" category="software" disambig="Open-weight language model family"/\>.

Model selection should consider:

- License and permitted commercial use.
- Memory requirements.
- Inference speed.
- Language performance.
- Accuracy on structured operational summaries.
- Hallucination rates.
- Hardware compatibility.

A smaller model that consistently produces useful summaries is preferable to a larger model that makes unsupported claims.

### 15.4 AI must provide traceability

This is essential.

If the AI produces the following statement:

"Two assignments remain outstanding."

The user should be able to inspect which two assignments support that conclusion.

AI should not invent:

- Incident details.
- People involved.
- Response outcomes.
- Resource availability.
- Team acknowledgements.
- Completion dates.

For important reports, generated statements should reference the underlying records.

### 15.5 AI fallback behaviour

If the model is unavailable:

- The application continues working.
- Reports can still be generated from templates.
- Dashboard statistics remain functional.
- Users can manually write summaries.
- No operational record becomes inaccessible because AI is unavailable.

This is what makes AI an enhancement rather than a dependency.

## 16\. Security and governance

This project could eventually handle sensitive humanitarian operational information. Security cannot be postponed until after the product becomes popular.

### 16.1 Authentication

Implement:

- Password hashing using a suitable modern password-hashing algorithm.
- Session expiration.
- Secure password reset procedures.
- Role-based permissions.
- Account deactivation.
- Administrative access controls.

Never store passwords in plain text.

### 16.2 Authorization

Permissions should be checked on the backend, not only by hiding buttons in the interface.

For example:

| Action | Coordinator | Team leader | Observer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Create response | Yes | Configurable | No |
| Assign team | Yes | No | No |
| Submit update | Yes | Yes | No |
| View authorized response | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Close response | Authorized coordinator | Configurable | No |
| Manage users | Administrator | No | No |

These are proposed permissions, not Red Cross policy.

### 16.3 Audit logs

Record important events:

- Login and logout.
- Response creation.
- Assignment creation.
- Assignment changes.
- Status changes.
- Support requests.
- Report exports.
- Administrative changes.

Each event should record the actor, timestamp, entity and action.

Audit logs should be protected against ordinary user modification. They should not contain unnecessary sensitive information.

### 16.4 Data protection

The Kenyan Data Protection Act and relevant regulations should inform the design.

Before any organizational pilot:

- Establish the lawful purpose of processing.
- Determine whether personal information is necessary.
- Define access restrictions.
- Establish retention periods.
- Review data-subject rights and organizational responsibilities.
- Determine whether a data protection impact assessment is required.
- Obtain appropriate authorization.

Consult the organization's data protection personnel where applicable.

### 16.5 Safeguarding boundaries

This deserves a separate warning.

A general response coordination tool is not automatically suitable for sensitive safeguarding reports, sexual exploitation and abuse allegations, child protection cases or confidential complaints.

Such cases may require specialized reporting channels, restricted personnel, referral processes and strict confidentiality.

CaseLoop should initially exclude them.

### 16.6 Intellectual property and open-source governance

Before publishing code, clarify:

- Who owns the code developed during the attachment.
- Whether company equipment or organizational resources are involved.
- Whether any organizational documents or workflows have been incorporated.
- Whether a supervisor must authorize publication.

# 40\. Closing vision

I want to leave you with one final thought.

There are actually three projects contained in your ambition:

**CaseLoop:** A useful humanitarian coordination application.

**The Digital Innovation Lab:** A way to preserve technical knowledge and give future attachment students something meaningful to work on.

**Your entrepreneurial development:** An opportunity to learn product development, architecture, user research, deployment, open-source collaboration and eventually commercial software development.

They can reinforce one another, but they should not be confused with one another.

The branch does not have to become a technology company. Management does not have to abandon its existing software. The Governor does not have to change how he coordinates teams. And AI does not have to be installed for CaseLoop to be useful.

If the prototype proves useful, the organization can decide whether to investigate it further.

If it doesn't, the lab can still preserve the knowledge and the next student can improve it.

If you eventually develop an independent company, that should happen through a clear ownership arrangement and a separate business foundation.

\<box border radius="lg" padding={3} gap={2}\> \<title size="xl"\>The guiding principle\</title\> \*\*Build something that is useful without being necessary, complementary without being disruptive, and extensible without being complicated.\*\*
